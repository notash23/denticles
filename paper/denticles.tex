\abstract{The drag reduction properties of shark denticles have been studied for several decades. Previous work suggests that these tiny scale-like structures reduce drag by reducing turbulent crossflow and lifting streamwise vortices. The goal of this study is to understand how different shark species denticles reduce drag. We first created a script that generates distinct denticles using the key feature dimensions. We then ran a Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) the fluid flow around these denticles on an airfoil and compared the drag reduction with Reynolds number of $10^5$ to $10^6$. We used a SST $k-\omega$ model for the turbulence. (We will also add the results here once we have completed the simulation)}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:1}

As a body moves through a fluid, it experiences a force along the motion of the fluid. This is called drag. Reducing drag is usually the goal while designing a vehicle. Despite the advancements, it still play a prevalent role in a body's dynamics. In fact, it is so relevant with trading ships that it contributes to around 70\% of the resistive forces \cite{lindholdt_effects_2015}. Reducing this figure can directly contribute to lowered shipping and transport costs.

Reif among others have established that shark skins can reduce drag \cite{reif_morphogenesis_1982}. These are shark denticles: tiny scale-like structures that prevent fluids from detaching from their surface. These structures that are on the submillimeter scale seems to reduce drag by up to 9.9\% \cite{bechert_experiments_1997}. However, further experiments showed drag reduction of up to 25.4\% \cite{guo_experimental_2023}.

While experiments have shown how shark denticles reduce drag, there are also theories that suggest how they do so. Near the surface of the skin, the fluid flowing along mean velocity stays attached. However, the fluid might get detached and create turbulent eddies\cite{bechert_experiments_1997}. Berchert suggests that the shark denticles have riblets that stop these eddies and rectify the fluid along the mean velocity keeping the flow attached \cite{guo_experimental_2023}. This then reduces shear-stresses and resultantly drag.

Most experiments and simulations, however, simplify the denticles that simple grooves. While there were effort to replicate denticles as a three-dimensional structure, they base themselves on one species of shark: \textit{Isurus oxyrinchus}. Despite being the fastest shark, there is merit to studying other species' denticles \cite{graybill_experimental_2024}.

By referring to Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and micro-CT scan models of denticles from different shark species, we created denticles models for shortfin mako (\textit{Isurus oxyrinchus}), smooth dogfish (\textit{Mustelus canis}), blacktip (\textit{Carcharhinus limbatus}), and white shark (\textit{Carcharodon carcharias}) \cite{ankhelyi_diversity_2018}. We created the models using the measurements of key features such as the crown length, crown width, and ridge depth. We then compared the drag reduction ($\eta$) by simulating the fluid flow using the SST $k-\omega$ model at different fluid speeds.

We studied the fluid flow around the airfoil design developed by the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA). One such airfoil is NACA 0012. NACA 0012 airfoils are widely used in marine engineering, aviation, and machinery research to due its simple geometry \cite{guo_experimental_2023}\cite{zhang_numerical_2018}. We placed a row of denticles on a NACA 0012 airfoil.

\section{Related Work and Background}
\label{sec:2}

The main motivation behind moving in this direction comes from the review paper from Graybill's review which remarked that most studies involved only shortfin mako's denticles.

\begin{quotation}
    One promising area of interest is to explore different variations of shark denticles, taking inspiration from diverse model shark species and denticle morphologies on different locations of the body \cite{graybill_experimental_2024}.
\end{quotation}

Evidently, both experiments and simulations either observe shortfin mako shark's denticles.

\textbf{Theory} A suggested explanation for why denticles helps with reducing drag is by mitigating fluid flowing perpendicular to the mean velocity which is described as turbulent crossflow by Berchert \cite{bechert_experiments_1997}. However, this does not describe the full picture. Another concept that might contribute to this effect is the riblets lifting streamwise vortices \cite{graybill_experimental_2024}.

\textbf{Experiments} Previous studies usually simplify denticle structures to riblet channels on surfaces \cite{zhang_characteristics_2020}. It was found that even these riblets caused a drag reduction of up to 10\% \cite{bechert_experiments_1997}. They use time-resolved particle image velocimetry (TR-PIV) to track the fluid flow \cite{lee_decrement_2008}. This means that there is a limit to how small the denticles can be.

\textbf{Simulations} Simulations usually allowed the use of geometries similar to denticles \cite{zhang_numerical_2018}. They also used large eddy simulation (LES) to model the fluid flow. This turbulence model capture effects of eddies by first simulating the larger scale turbulences and then modeling the smaller subgrid turbulences. Another study instead used SST $k-\omega$ -- a Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes model\cite{mawignon_optimization_2023}. This model accounts for all the turbulence by using an effective viscosity. It models the averaged effects of turbulence. \textbf{TODO: we will define LES and SST $k-\omega$ better}  We decided to use SST $k-\omega$ since it is a steady state model which is computationally less expensive than LES.

\textbf{Experimental limitations and current alternatives} Experiments are limited to scale because it is quite challenging to manufacture submillimeter denticles unless they are simplified to channels riblets \cite{graybill_experimental_2024}. However, Zhang et al. successfully replicated sharkskins and measured drag reduction on this skin. They obtained 24.6\% -- similar to recent simulations \cite{zhang_high-precision_2011}\cite{guo_experimental_2023}. However one would need to have sharkskin to recreate the structure.

In this study, we investigated the drag coefficient of shark denticles using a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation in OpenFOAM \cite{openfoam}. The denticle geometry was modeled based on micro-Computed Tomography (CT) scans to ensure realistic representation in the domain. First, we validated the simulation workflow by comparing lift and drag values and drag rates by replicating case (a) of the paper from Guo et. al. \cite{guo_experimental_2023}. We created the mesh of the geometry in OpenFOAM using the \textit{snappyHexMesh} \cite{snappyHexMesh} (might be Issa's work, and Spalding \& Pantakar) utility targeting a near-wall $y^+\approx 1 $ to catch boundary layer behavior and used \textit{simpleFoam} as a solver \cite{simpleFoam} (TODO: We might need rephrase this and next sentence). We modeled the flow as steady-state and incompressible flow with the SST $k-\omega$ turbulence model. One of the main objectives of this study is to create a simulation that is as close to reality as possible. For this reason, water was used for the fluid with density, $\rho = 1000 \text{kg}/\text{m}^3$, and kinematic viscosity, $\mu = 1\times 10^{-6} \text{m}^2/\text{s}$ at $20 ^\circ C$. Because the simulation ran in steady state, we utilized simpleFoam as a solver. Then, post-processing of the result was executed in ParaView to obtain the lift and drag forces.

\subsection{Validation of simulation}
\label{subsec:3}

The NACA 0012 airfoil has a symmetrical cross-section with a maximum relative thickness of $12\%$ and an aspect cord ratio of $W/L=2$, where $L$ represents the chord length, and $W$ denotes the span length. The dimensions of the NACA 0012 airfoil in this research are $L=408$ and $W=200$. While real shortfin mako denticles are on the micrometer scale, the simulation that we are referencing by Gao et. al. set up a physical experiment and encountered today's manufacturing limits. To overcome these limitations, the denticle geometry was scaled 60 times. To maintain the same conditions as this benchmark study, we adopted the same $60\times$ scaled-up denticle dimensions. Following the specification for Case (a) in \cite{guo_experimental_2023}, we positioned a row of shortfin mako shark denticles along the NACA 0012 airfoil at 0.1L from the leading edge as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:3}. However, Gao et. al. evaluated various flow velocities and angles of attack. For simplicity, we selected an inlet velocity, $U = 0.5 \text{m/s}$, and a 0-degree angle of attack. The benchmark study calculated both drag coefficient ($C_D$) and the drag reduction rate. To achieve this, Guo et al. also performed simulations on a smooth airfoil which means airfoil without denticles attached on it. Therefore, we replicated both the smooth airfoil and denticle-equipped airfoil from Case (a).

\subsection{Postprocessing}
\label{subsec:4}
Post-processing and data extraction were conducted using ParaView. To evaluate the lift and drag forces acting on the model, we extracted pressure value. Lift was determined from the multiplication of pressure and viscous shear forces acting perpendicular to the freestream direction, while drag was computed from the forces aligned parallel to the flow vector. The drag coefficient ($C_D$) and the lift coefficient ($C_L$) were calculated using extracted following the methodology in \cite{guo_experimental_2023}

\begin{equation}
    C_D = \frac{2 F_D}{\rho A U^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    C_L = \frac{2 F_L}{\rho A U^2}
\end{equation}

where $F_D$ and $F_L$ are the total drag and lift forces, respectively, $\rho$ is the fluid density ($\rho = 1\text{ kg/m}^3$), $U$ is the freestream velocity, and $A$ is the cross sectional area, defined as $A = W \cdot L$ Furthermore, the formula for the drag reduction rate is structured as follows:

\begin{equation}
     \eta = \frac{C_{D_s} - C_{D_m}}{C_{D_s}}\times 100\%
\end{equation}

where $C_{D_s}$ is the drag coefficient of smooth airfoil while $C_{D_m}$ is the drag coefficient of airfoil with row of denticle attached on it.

Because Blacktip shark denticles are ???? times larger than those of the Shortfin Mako while providing superior drag reduction, they present a distinct manufacturing advantage.
