In this study, we investigated the drag coefficient of shark denticles using a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation in OpenFOAM. 
The denticle geometry was modeled based on micro-CT scans to ensure realistic representation in the domain. 
First, we validated the simulation workflow by comparing lift and drag values and drag rates by replicating case (a) of the paper from Guo et. al. \cite{guo_experimental_2023}. 
We created the mesh of the geometry in OpenFOAM using the snappyHexMesh utility targeting a near-wall $y^+\approx 1 $ to catch boundary layer behavior. 
We modeled the flow as steady-state and incompressible flow with the $k-\omega \ SST$ turbulence model. 
One of the main objectives of this study is to create a simulation that is as close to reality as possible. 
For this reason, water was used for the fluid with density, $\rho = 1000 kg/m^3$, and kinematic viscosity, $\mu = 1e-6 m^2/s$ at $20 ^\circ C$. 
Because the simulation ran in steady state, we utilized simpleFoam as a solver. 
Then, post-processing of the result was executed in ParaView to obtain the lift and drag forces.

\subsection{Validation of simulation}
\label{subsec:3}

The NACA 0012 (National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics) airfoils are widely used in marine engineering, aviation, and machinery research to due it’s simple geometry {cite the papers that used it}. 
The airfoil has a symmetrical cross-section with a maximum relative thickness of $12\%$ and an aspect cord ratio of W/L=2, where L represents the chord length, and W denotes the span length. 
The dimensions of the NACA 0012 airfoil in this research are L=408 and W=200. 
While real shortfin mako denticles are on the micrometer scale, the simulation that we are referencing by Gao et. al. \cite{guo_experimental_2023} set up a physical experiment and encountered today's manufacturing limits. 
To overcome these limitations, the denticle geometry was scaled 60 times.  
To maintain the same conditions as this benchmark study \cite{guo_experimental_2023}, we adopted the same $60\times$ scaled-up denticle dimensions.    
Following the specification for Case (a) in \cite{guo_experimental_2023}, we positioned a row of shortfin mako shark denticles along the NACA 0012 airfoil at 0.1L from the leading edge. 
However, Gao et. al. evaluated various flow velocities and angles of attack. 
For simplicity, we selected an inlet velocity, U = 0.5 m/s, and a 0-degree angle of attack.
The benchmark study calculated both drag coefficient ($C_D$) and the drag rate percentage.
To achieve this purpose, Guo et. al. also performed simulations on a smooth airfoil which means airfoil without denticles attached on it. 
Therefore, we replicated both the smooth airfoil and denticle-equipped airfoil from Case (a).

\subsection{Postprocessing}
\label{subsec:4}
Post-processing and data extraction were conducted using ParaView. 
To evaluate the lift and drag forces acting on the model, we extracted pressure value. 
Lift was determined from the multiplication of pressure and viscous shear forces acting perpendicular to the freestream direction, while drag was computed from the forces aligned parallel to the flow vector.
The drag coefficient ($C_D$) and the lift coefficient ($C_L$) were calculated using extracted following the methodology in \cite{guo_experimental_2023}

\begin{equation}
    C_D = \frac{2 F_D}{\rho A U^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    C_L = \frac{2 F_L}{\rho A U^2}
\end{equation}

where $F_D$ and $F_L$ are the total drag and lift forces, respectively, $\rho$ is the fluid density ($\rho = 1\text{ kg/m}^3$), $U$ is the freestream velocity, and $A$ is the cross sectional area, defined as $A = W \cdot L$
Furthermore, the formula for the drag reduction rate is structured as follows:

\begin{equation}
     \eta = \frac{C_D_s - C_D_m}{C_D_s}*100\%
\end{equation}

where $C_D_s$ is the drag coefficient of smooth airfoil while $C_D_m$ is the drag coefficient of airfoil with row of denticle attached on it.
